# Claude nearly matches markets, rarely profits

People have used Claude on Polymarket in two different ways, and only one of them is what this project wants. Nearly every viral "Claude bot" is a case of Claude writing code for a mechanical strategy (latency arbitrage, sports arbitrage, copy trading, weather-model comparison); the headline returns are anonymous or unverifiable, and Polymarket closed the main mechanical edge with taker fees during 2026. Where Claude or another large language model (LLM) actually makes the forecasting decisions, the independent real-money record is negative: six frontier models given $10,000 each all lost on Kalshi (average -22.6%), and the one Claude result retrieved is **Claude Opus 4.5 at -25.9% over 886 trades** ([arXiv 2604.07355](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07355v1)). The same literature shows frontier models now sit close to superforecasters and to the market price on accuracy, and the best-supported positive finding is small: averaging an LLM forecast with the market price beats the market alone (Brier score 0.106 against 0.111) ([arXiv 2511.07678](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07678)). That points to one defensible design: treat the market price as the prior and Claude as a small adjustment, trade rarely, enter with resting limit orders, hold to settlement, and let deterministic code hold every limit. For a US resident the lawful venues are Polymarket US and Kalshi; the international polymarket.com site is off limits, and state eligibility on Polymarket US is contested in secondary sources for some states (Illinois is one). The recommended path is at least eight weeks of forward paper trading against pre-registered gates, then $200 to $500 of live money at roughly one-eighth Kelly with a 2% cap per market. The honest expectation is a result near break-even before model costs, with total loss of the stake possible mainly through operational or platform failure. Almost every figure below comes from search-result summaries and must be re-checked before real money is used; only the API facts were read first-hand from official software development kit (SDK) source.

## Polymarket US and Kalshi are the only lawful venues

### The international site is closed to US residents

Polymarket runs two separate exchanges. Polymarket International (polymarket.com) uses crypto collateral on Polygon and still excludes US persons. Its Terms of Use reportedly bar the use of a VPN to get around geographic restrictions ([tradetheoutcome.com](https://www.tradetheoutcome.com/polymarket-section-2-1-4/)), and on October 27, 2026 the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) was reported to have **reopened a probe into whether Polymarket kept US users off the offshore platform**, as its 2022 settlement required ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/27/cftc-probes-polymarket-over-2022-settlement.html)). The same coverage notes a US soldier charged in April 2026 whose indictment says he reached the platform through a VPN ([US News/Reuters](https://www.usnews.com/news/top-news/articles/2026-10-27/exclusive-us-agency-is-again-probing-if-polymarket-broke-2022-settlement-sources-says)). Polymarket's own `agents` repository states that the terms prohibit US persons from trading through the interface or the API, agents included ([Polymarket/agents](https://github.com/Polymarket/agents)). For this project the international site is at most a source of read-only research data, and whether its public data endpoints are reachable from a US address was not verified.

Polymarket US (polymarket.us) is the CFTC-regulated exchange operated by QCX LLC. It launched in December 2025, dropped its waitlist in May 2026, and by November 2026 lists sports, politics, economics, crypto and culture contracts for US residents aged 18 and over ([rg.org](https://rg.org/predictions/review/polymarket)). It holds customer money as US dollars, requires identity verification before deposit, and issues API keys only to verified account holders. Its keys do not work on the international exchange and the two venues do not share markets or liquidity.

### State eligibility varies: two Midwestern examples

No official state list was retrieved, and the third-party lists conflict. Illinois and Iowa were examined in detail as examples of a contested state and a quieter one. The lists conflict sharply on Illinois. One November 2026 guide reports a written statement from Polymarket US that the app is available in "every state except Nevada" and adds that "no order currently blocks access in Illinois; that could change" ([predictionmarketspicks.com](https://predictionmarketspicks.com/learn/is-polymarket-legal-in-illinois)). Another puts the exclusions at Nevada and Utah ([Covers](https://www.covers.com/politics/is-kalshi-polymarket-midterm-elections-legal-in-my-state)). Several mid-2026 guides instead list **Illinois among seven to nine excluded states** ([pm.wiki](https://pm.wiki/tr/learn/how-to-use-polymarket-in-the-us); [next.io](https://next.io/prediction-markets/polymarket/legal/)). No source lists Iowa as excluded, which is weak positive evidence only. The decisive test for either state is the eligibility and identity check inside the app.

Both states are active legal battlegrounds, and the fights centre on sports contracts. The Illinois Gaming Board sent Polymarket a letter dated January 27, 2026 calling its sports contracts unlicensed wagering ([predictionmarketspicks.com](https://predictionmarketspicks.com/learn/is-polymarket-legal-in-illinois)), and on April 2, 2026 the Justice Department and CFTC sued Illinois to assert exclusive federal jurisdiction ([NPR Illinois](https://www.nprillinois.org/illinois/2026-04-03/trump-administration-sues-illinois-over-states-attempts-to-regulate-prediction-markets)). In Iowa, Kalshi sued the attorney general pre-emptively on March 11, 2026 ([Iowa Capital Dispatch](https://iowacapitaldispatch.com/2026/03/17/company-seeks-injunction-to-block-state-oversight-of-event-prediction-wagering/)), and a federal judge reportedly denied its request for a preliminary injunction, on a date the researchers could not confirm ([SportsBettingDime](https://www.sportsbettingdime.com/news/industry/federal-judge-denies-kalshi-injunction-request-in-iowa/)). The appeals courts are split: the Third Circuit sided with Kalshi in April 2026, while the Ninth and Sixth Circuits sided with the states on sports contracts, the Sixth on September 25, 2026 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/appeals-court-rules-states-can-regulate-sports-prediction-markets.html)). Neither the Seventh Circuit (Illinois) nor the Eighth (Iowa) had ruled in the sources found. The practical risk for a user on a regulated venue is a sudden state restriction that forces positions to be closed, as with Michigan sports contracts on Polymarket US ending November 25, 2026 ([rg.org](https://rg.org/predictions/review/polymarket)). A bot that avoids sports carries less of that risk.

Kalshi is the established alternative. It was reported available in all 50 states as of October 28, 2026, with sports contracts blocked in Massachusetts, Nevada, Michigan, Utah and Washington, so neither Illinois nor Iowa was on the blocked list ([predictmarkets.com](https://predictmarkets.com/kalshi/)). No source confirmed either state by name.

### Fees punish small takers and spare makers

Neither venue is free. Both charge takers a fee proportional to price times one minus price, so the fee per contract peaks at 50 cents, and both charge makers nothing on standard markets.

Three consequences follow for a small account. At the entry tier Polymarket US costs a taker about 36% more than Kalshi ([CCN](https://www.ccn.com/news/crypto/polymarket-us-fees-36-percent-higher-kalshi/)). A taker who enters and exits before settlement on Polymarket US pays roughly 9.5% of stake in fees at mid prices before any spread, so early exits are ruinous and holding to settlement or resting maker orders changes the arithmetic. And fees as a share of stake are highest on cheap contracts: at 10 cents the entry-tier Polymarket US fee is about 8.5% of the amount paid. Volume rebates start at $200,000 to $250,000 of monthly volume and are irrelevant here.

### A few hundred dollars is the practical floor

Hard minimums are tiny, so cost and noise set the floor, not order size. Official minimum order and tick sizes for Polymarket US and Kalshi were not found. On the researchers' reasoning, **$250 to $500 is a sensible floor for a live experiment with $5 to $25 positions**, $1,000 to $2,000 allows 40 or more small positions, and below about $100 the result would be dominated by rounding and minimum sizes. These are judgments, not sourced figures. Kalshi pays interest only above a $250 average balance. Automated trading on one's own account is an expected use on both venues, and no rule found singles out AI agents; the binding rules are the bans on wash trading, spoofing and self-matching, and on Kalshi a developer agreement limiting API use to your own trading ([traadence.com](https://www.traadence.com/blog/kalshi-automated-trading-bot-rules); [BeInCrypto](https://beincrypto.com/polymarket-kalshi-insider-trading-rules/)).

Tax treatment is unsettled. The Internal Revenue Service had issued no guidance on event contracts as of mid to late 2026, so gambling, capital-gain and Section 1256 treatments are all argued ([monacocpa.cpa](https://www.monacocpa.cpa/prediction-market-tax)). If the activity is treated as gambling, a 2026 law change caps loss deductions at 90% of losses, which can create tax in a break-even year ([Keeper](https://www.keepertax.com/posts/are-gambling-losses-tax-deductible)). Kalshi reportedly issues no form that reports trading profit ([coselite.com](https://coselite.com/blog/kalshi-taxes)), and what Polymarket US issues was not found. The bot should therefore keep a complete timestamped ledger of fills, fees and settlements, and a tax professional should confirm classification before filing. Illinois and Iowa state treatment was not researched.

The viral numbers travel one channel: an anonymous X post, then a crypto news site, then exchange news feeds. Repetition across those outlets is one source, not corroboration. One software firm that examined an "$868,862 wallet" claim found it unverifiable with the source video gone, and treats such posts "as claims, not verified results" ([Valletta Software](https://vallettasoftware.com/blog/post/polymarket-trading-bot-claude-fable-5)). A veteran trader's rebuttal of a "Claude earned $10k overnight" story makes the central point: a human chose everything, Claude wrote copy-trading code, and the profit came from copying traders who happened to win ([Medium](https://medium.com/@info_38412/claude-didnt-earn-10k-overnight-and-that-isn-t-how-markets-work-b2bb2717aa76)).

The mechanical edge these bots relied on has been attacked by rule change. Polymarket added taker fees to 15-minute crypto markets in January 2026, in coverage explicitly tied to bots exploiting the lag between Polymarket and exchange prices, and extended fees to most categories by March 30, 2026 ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/384461/polymarket-adds-taker-fees-to-15-minute-crypto-markets-to-fund-liquidity-rebates); [Pine Analytics](https://pineanalytics.substack.com/p/polymarket-fee-rollout)).

The honest accounts are mostly failures, and they are mundane. An investment writer's bot showed a 98.8% win rate buying 97-cent contracts in a no-money run, then took a live account **from $400 to $134 in two weeks** ([Purpose Investments](https://www.purposeinvest.com/thoughtful/what-i-learned-losing-money-to-ai-in-a-prediction-market)). A months-long live strategy with a public scoreboard "lost money every month" and was shut down ([falsifylab](https://falsifylab.substack.com/p/kill-polymarket)). A small-bankroll agent "lost almost everything", partly because minimum order sizes cripple tiny accounts ([Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/i-actually-gave-an-ai-money-to-trade-on-polymarket-heres-what-nobody-on-twitter-wants-you-to-know/)). A researcher's Claude Code agent on Kalshi political markets "often seemed to overinterpret crude news articles without appreciating the broader context" ([Free Systems](https://freesystems.substack.com/p/can-ai-reason-about-politics-i-built)). The researchers found no open-source LLM-forecaster project with a public wallet and sustained positive profit, though with page fetches blocked that absence is not proof.

Aggregate data explains who does win. A Bloomberg analysis relayed by secondary outlets found 823 bot-like accounts earned $131 million while everyone else collectively lost the same amount, and that bot-like wallets were slightly less accurate than humans (about 52% against 55%) while profiting from smaller, more frequent, better-executed trades ([KuCoin flash](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/most-polymarket-traders-report-losses-while-a-small-group-of-bots-earns-131m)). Winners are market makers, arbitrageurs and latency traders. No study isolates LLM-forecasting wallets, and nothing shows them profitable as a class. A Claude trader should be judged on the price it pays against fair value, not on its hit rate.

## LLMs reach near-parity on accuracy and still lose money trading

### Forecast accuracy has closed most of the gap

On proper scoring measures (the Brier score, where lower is better and always saying 50% scores 0.25), frontier models moved from clearly behind human experts in 2024 to roughly level in 2026. ForecastBench launched in October 2024 with superforecasters at 0.093 and the best model at 0.111 ([FRI on X](https://x.com/Research_FRI/status/1841070675382902861)); in July 2026 its organisers reported that AI had "likely reached parity" with a nominal, statistically insignificant lead ([FRI Substack](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/ai-models-have-likely-reached-parity)). On Metaculus, professional forecasters beat every bot by wide margins through mid-2025, but in spring 2026 a team of ten professionals beat the ten best bots by a margin that was not statistically significant, although nine of the ten individual professionals still beat every individual bot ([FutureEval Spring results](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/wZBbDqzfBjYG58CxK/futureeval-spring-results-pros-beat-bots-but-the-gap-is)). Critics note the superforecaster baseline is a frozen 2024 snapshot and the comparison is extrapolated ([Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.substack.com/p/not-so-fast)).

Claude's relative strength is calibration, not profit. Claude Opus 4.5 was the best-calibrated of five models on 300 Kalshi questions and the only one to beat simple base rates ([arXiv 2512.16030](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16030)). On the Prophet Arena live board, Claude Opus 5.5 sits one rank below the Kalshi market baseline, at 0.9458 against 0.9460, a statistical tie ([Prophet Arena leaderboard](https://www.prophetarena.co/leaderboard)). Anthropic itself publishes no event-forecasting score or calibration curve for any Claude model, so calibration has to be measured in-house on forward forecasts.

### The market price remains the hardest baseline

"Parity with superforecasters" and "beats the market" are different bars, and the market won wherever both were scored. On ForecastBench's market questions, 442 of 527 forecasters scored worse than the market price they were shown, and baseline models scored barely above chance when the price was hidden ([EA Forum, "The Copier Line"](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/8YuG9EuhyYqaNBt7F/the-copier-line-what-forecastbench-s-market-scores-actually)). On 251 live Polymarket questions in 2025 the market scored 0.170 while Claude Opus scored 0.221, the weakest frontier model in that vendor-run table ([Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/LightningRodLabs/foresight-32B/blob/main/README.md)). Bridgewater's multi-agent AIA Forecaster trailed market consensus on liquid markets, 0.1258 against 0.1106 ([arXiv 2511.07678](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07678)).

Polymarket prices are themselves well calibrated. An independent analysis of 7,661 resolved markets found Brier 0.025 a day before resolution and 0.042 thirty days out ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Hruc6Gwo3vBZFGb6v/empirical-calibration-of-polymarket-analysis-of-7-661)). The documented departures are structural: prices sit too close to 50% at long horizons and in political markets, with a calibration slope near 1.3 beyond a month ([arXiv 2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19520)).

### Trading results are negative wherever an outsider ran the test

The pattern across independent tests is consistent. In Prediction Arena all six models lost real money on Kalshi while the same cohort averaged -1.1% on Polymarket; the most selective trader made 18 trades with a 71.4% win rate and the most active Claude run made 886 trades and lost 25.9% ([arXiv 2604.07355](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07355v1)). In PolyBench five of seven models lost in simulated trading against real order books while stating average confidence above 0.80 ([arXiv 2604.14199](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14199)). On KellyBench, a season of sports betting, every frontier model lost, with Claude Opus 4.6 best at launch at about -11% and Claude Opus 4.7 later leading at -3.7% ([General Reasoning](https://www.gr.inc/releases/introducing-kellybench)). In the Prophet Arena paper every model's average return was below break-even even where its Brier score matched the market ([arXiv 2510.17638](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.17638v2)).

The positive records come from the systems' own builders and cover one to five months. The most instructive is FutureSearch's: the same strategy on the same venue showed +49.8% on paper and +5.9% on the real account ([FutureSearch Markets](https://markets.futuresearch.ai/)). That 44-point gap is the best available measure of how much simulated fills overstate returns, and it means any paper profit is an upper bound.

The losses do not come from gross forecasting incompetence. They come from selection (trading where the model disagrees with a better-informed market), overconfidence, sizing and costs. Being allowed not to trade is valuable: the gap between forced engagement with a fixed Kalshi question set and free discovery on Polymarket was more than 20 points in the same study, though its authors could not separate market selection from pricing.

### Five techniques have measured benefit; prompt wording does not

The measured gains rank roughly as follows. A stronger base model matters most: Metaculus concluded that having a state-of-the-art model mattered more than scaffolding in its mid-2025 tournament ([Metaculus Q2 2025](https://www.metaculus.com/aib/2025/q2/)). Agentic multi-round search comes next, cutting Brier from 0.1230 to 0.11435 where single-shot search gave only marginal gains, and going from one forecast to ten independent runs lowers Brier substantially with diminishing returns beyond ten ([arXiv 2511.07678](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07678)). Statistical recalibration fitted to a system's own history improved Metaculus bots by 0.016 Brier on binary questions ([Metaculus synthesis](https://www.metaculus.com/notebooks/43363/ai-forecasting-in-2026/)), but a fixed-coefficient version made things worse in another paper ([arXiv 2611.17735](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17735)), so the correction must be fitted on the bot's own resolved forecasts and never borrowed. **Blending with the market price is the largest single improvement where a price exists**: 0.106 for the blend against 0.111 for the market alone ([arXiv 2511.07678](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07678)), though secondary sources quote slightly different figures for the same comparison, and simple averaging of human and machine forecasts beat letting the model update on the human number itself ([arXiv 2402.19379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379)).

Prompt engineering adds almost nothing. A test of 38 prompts across four models, including two Claude models, found that no prompt significantly beat a minimal control after correction, and that Bayesian-reasoning prompts could reduce accuracy ([arXiv 2506.01578](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.01578)). Evidence on reasoning effort is mixed and comes only from GPT models.

### The failure modes are known and specific

All five models on the Kalshi calibration benchmark were overconfident ([arXiv 2512.16030](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16030)). Retrieved text is the sharpest danger: one planted article per question flipped 56% of a retrieval-augmented forecaster's forecasts, and the three defences tested each had a cheap bypass ([arXiv 2609.22246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22246)). Models aggregate information more slowly than markets as resolution nears ([arXiv 2510.17638](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.17638)), and added news context pushed models into momentum-driven wrong calls on financial questions ([arXiv 2511.18394](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18394v1)).

Retrospective backtests of an LLM are close to worthless. Training cutoffs are a guideline and not a guarantee, search-engine date filters leak, and question sets built with hindsight constrain the answer ([arXiv 2506.00723](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00723v1)). The only clean evidence is forward paper trading with timestamps.

Evidence on where LLMs do least badly is thin and partly contradictory. The Prophet Arena summary credits models at long horizons and markets near resolution, while an earlier study found its system worse than the crowd far from resolution and marginally better only where the crowd was uncertain, between 30% and 70% ([arXiv 2402.18563](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.18563)). No public study tests a rule of the form "trade only when model and price differ by more than X" out of sample, which is the experiment this project would itself be running.

## Slow-bot edges are structural; speed edges are closed

Independent datasets agree on the base rate. Galaxy Research found 69.2% of about 2.9 million retail Polymarket accounts below break-even, with a median loss of about $3 ([Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/galaxy-research-polymarket-retail-losses/)). An academic study of 588 million trades found the top 1% of profitable users capture 76.5% of profits, and identified the mechanism: winners provide liquidity with limit orders, losers take liquidity with market orders, and longshot betting drives the losses ([CEPR DP21615](https://cepr.org/publications/dp21615)). Kalshi shows the same transfer, with takers losing almost 32% on average and makers about 10% in one study ([VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/economics-kalshi-prediction-market)). Most of these datasets pre-date the 2026 fees, so taker outcomes today are worse. They describe the international Polymarket book and Kalshi; **no evidence was found on whether Polymarket US has different inefficiencies**.

Two findings shape the market filter. First, insider trading is documented and concentrated. In 2025 and 2026 wallets repeatedly traded ahead of non-public information in geopolitical, military, corporate-announcement and award markets; one analysis identified 152 wallets with a 97.2% win rate on longshot military bets, and the first federal charges followed in 2026 ([Yahoo News/Reuters](https://ca.news.yahoo.com/exclusive-more-150-polymarket-wallets-100543612.html)). In those markets a cheap contract being bought aggressively is informed flow, not a biased retail longshot, and they are the worst place to rest a passive order. Second, liquidity is extremely concentrated. A November 2026 snapshot of the 300 deepest international events found median spreads near 1 cent in politics, crypto and economy markets, yet 87% of contracts had no trades in 24 hours ([pmcrypto](https://pmcrypto.blog/data/liquidity/)). A 600-market panel found median quoted spreads of roughly 4% of price in the 40 to 60 cent range and 13% to 18% below 10 cents ([arXiv 2604.24366](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24366)). Thin markets are likelier to be mispriced and harder to exit, so positions there must be sized to be held to resolution.

The fee structure and the bias literature point the same way. Fees as a share of stake fall hardest on longshot and mid-price takers, lightly on favourite buyers and not at all on makers. The documented edges are of the order of 1% to 5% per position before costs, and the main value of evidence-based rules is avoiding the well-documented retail loss channels.

## Polymarket US is a plain signed REST exchange

### Polymarket US API, read from the SDK source

The official Python SDK is `polymarket-us` (version 2.4.1, Python 3.10 or later), with a TypeScript twin ([polymarket-us-python](https://github.com/Polymarket/polymarket-us-python)). It is a conventional custodial exchange API with no wallets, no on-chain signing and no crypto collateral. Public market data lives on `https://gateway.polymarket.us` and needs no key; trading and portfolio calls go to `https://api.polymarket.us` ([SDK client.py](https://github.com/Polymarket/polymarket-us-python/blob/main/polymarket_us/client.py)).

Authentication uses Ed25519 signatures. The key ID is a UUID and the secret is a base64 Ed25519 private key, generated at polymarket.us/developer. Each request carries three headers: `X-PM-Access-Key`, `X-PM-Timestamp` in milliseconds, and `X-PM-Signature`, a base64 signature over the timestamp, method and path concatenated ([SDK auth.py](https://github.com/Polymarket/polymarket-us-python/blob/main/polymarket_us/auth.py)). Third-party guides add that keys are issued only after identity verification in the app and that the secret is shown once ([quantvps.com](https://www.quantvps.com/blog/polymarket-us-api-available)).

The public endpoints are enough to build a read-only client and a paper simulator. They include `GET /v1/events`, `GET /v1/markets`, `GET /v1/market/slug/{slug}`, `GET /v1/markets/{slug}/book`, `GET /v1/markets/{slug}/bbo` and `GET /v1/markets/{slug}/settlement`, with list calls taking `limit`, `offset` and `active`. Markets are addressed by slug. A book response is wrapped in `marketData` and holds `bids` and `offers` as price-and-quantity pairs plus a market `state`. Trading endpoints include `POST /v1/orders`, `GET /v1/orders/open`, `POST /v1/order/{id}/cancel`, `POST /v1/orders/open/cancel` to cancel everything, `GET /v1/portfolio/positions` and `GET /v1/account/balances`, with batch variants of up to 20 orders ([SDK orders.py](https://github.com/Polymarket/polymarket-us-python/blob/main/polymarket_us/resources/orders.py)).

An order names a `marketSlug`, an `intent` (buy or sell, long or short, on one instrument), a `type` (limit or market), a `price` object in USD, a `quantity` and a time-in-force (good-till-cancel, good-till-date, immediate-or-cancel or fill-or-kill). The order type also carries a `participateDontInitiate` field, which by its name is the maker-only control and should be confirmed in the docs. Two behaviours matter for safety. **`POST /v1/order/preview` gives a server-side dry run**, and the SDK never retries order placement automatically, "so a network blip cannot submit a duplicate order" ([SDK README](https://github.com/Polymarket/polymarket-us-python)).

What the SDK source does not show is exactly what a bot needs before going live: fees by tier, rate limits (one third-party guide says 20 requests per second per key ([agentbets.ai](https://agentbets.ai/guides/polymarket-us-api-guide/))), minimum order and tick sizes, whether any sandbox exists, how settlement and disputes work under the exchange rulebook, and the API terms of use. All of these sit on docs.polymarket.us, which was blocked.

### Kalshi API in brief

Kalshi offers a free retail API with token-bucket rate limits by tier. The basic tier allows roughly 20 reads and 10 order writes per second, and over-limit requests return HTTP 429 ([edgeoutcome.com](https://edgeoutcome.com/blog/2026-11-02-kalshi-api-rate-limits/)). That is far more than a slow bot needs. Kalshi's minimum order size, tick size and fee rounding rule were not verified.

### International mechanics, for read-only research only

The international stack matters here only as background and as a possible source of research data. Its public metadata API (`gamma-api.polymarket.com`) and order-book API (`clob.polymarket.com`) need no authentication for reads, and its market objects return `outcomes`, `outcomePrices` and `clobTokenIds` as JSON-encoded strings that must be parsed ([py-sdk market model](https://github.com/Polymarket/py-sdk/blob/main/src/polymarket/models/gamma/market.py)). Both official SDKs compute the taker fee as shares times rate times (price times one minus price) raised to an exponent, with rate and exponent read per market ([py-sdk market orders](https://github.com/Polymarket/py-sdk/blob/main/src/polymarket/_internal/actions/orders/market.py)).

The older `py-clob-client` library that most tutorials and Polymarket's own `agents` framework depend on is archived and described by Polymarket as "no longer functional" ([py-clob-client](https://github.com/Polymarket/py-clob-client)). The 2026 migration replaced the exchange contracts and the collateral token, so any 2025-era tutorial is obsolete. Resolution on the international site runs largely through the UMA oracle, whose disputes drove the worst trader losses of 2025 and 2026. None of this applies to an account on Polymarket US.

### Neither venue offers a paper mode, so the simulator is local

No official paper-trading mode exists on the international stack, and whether Polymarket US has a sandbox is unknown. The practical design is a local simulator fed by production read-only data. It should fill marketable orders by walking the recorded offer side of the book level by level, count a resting order as filled only when the market later trades through its price, apply the venue's fee formula, mark positions at the bid and not the mid, and settle from the venue's settlement data. The trading key should be absent from the process entirely in paper mode so that no code path can place a real order. Historical price series are available but historical order books are not, so the bot should start recording book snapshots on day one.

## Size at one-eighth Kelly and let code hold every limit

### Sizing shrinks the estimate, then the stake

For a binary contract bought at all-in cost c (ask price plus fee) with estimated probability q, the full Kelly stake is (q - c) / (1 - c) of the bankroll; this is the standard derivation. The literature is unanimous that an estimated probability must be bet at well below full Kelly. Baker and McHale show that stakes should be shrunk under parameter uncertainty to improve out-of-sample results ([INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/deca.2013.0271)), and MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba document that full Kelly can suffer heavy losses in a bad run while fractional Kelly trades growth for safety ([Berkeley-hosted PDF](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf)).

The researchers' recommended rule has two layers. First shrink Claude's probability toward the market price, using a weight w of 0.3 to 0.5 on Claude until the bot's own data says otherwise. Then bet one quarter of Kelly on that shrunk probability. Shrinking the probability by w is algebraically the same as betting w times Kelly, so the effective stake is about one-eighth Kelly. Their arithmetic shows why the margin matters: if the true edge is half the estimate, "full Kelly" is in fact twice Kelly and has zero long-run growth, while quarter Kelly stays growth-positive unless the edge is overstated more than eightfold. These are derived figures and valid only under the model's own assumptions.

The hard caps do more work than the formula. The recommended defaults are 2% of bankroll per market, 4% to 5% per event, 8% to 10% per cluster of positions that would lose together, no more than 40% to 50% of the bankroll deployed, and at most 15 to 25 positions. The community defaults found in open-source Claude bots (a 5% minimum edge, half Kelly and a 25% position cap) have no evidence behind them and are far too aggressive given measured overconfidence ([getclawkit](https://getclawkit.com/skills/official-srikanthbellary-polymarket-auto-trader)).

### Entry needs several cents of edge after costs

The recommended entry rule is a shrunk edge of at least 3 to 5 cents over all-in cost, inside a 15 to 85 cent price band. With a weight of 0.5 on Claude, that means the raw gap between Claude and the market must be roughly 8 to 10 cents. An upper bound matters as much: a gap above 20 to 25 cents in a liquid market is more likely model error or missing information than edge, and should trigger human review and no trade. These thresholds were assembled by the researchers from the cost components; no practitioner source states them.

Execution should use limit orders only, sized against displayed depth, resting passively where practical to avoid the taker fee. A resting order is a free option handed to faster traders, so the bot should cancel everything before scheduled news, on shutdown and on start-up, and should refuse to trade on an estimate older than 6 to 12 hours. The default is to hold to settlement. Price-based stop-losses do not suit binary contracts on thin books; exits should be driven by new information or by the portfolio-level breakers.

### Platform and state risk replace wallet risk on a regulated venue

Most of the dramatic platform losses of 2025 and 2026 happened on the international site: resolution disputes settled by concentrated token votes, as in the Strategy bitcoin-sale market where a late clarification reversed the plain reading ([Galaxy Research](https://www.galaxy.com/insights/research/strategy-bitcoin-sale-polymarket-resolution-dispute-may-2026)), and two front-end compromises, the second of which drained at least $2.73 million from 737 accounts on October 29, 2026 while the US exchange was unaffected ([Unchained](https://unchainedcrypto.com/polymarket-resumes-trading-vows-to-reimburse-traders-after-third-party-provider-compromise/)). A US account does not face oracle votes or stablecoin collateral. It faces rule and fee changes week to week, state restrictions that force close-outs, and an exchange rulebook that the researchers could not read.

The rule-reading lessons transfer to any venue. Nearly every notable dispute involved a subjective term ("suit", "invade", "ceasefire", "agree to a deal"), an unnamed resolution source, or ambiguity about whether an event or its announcement had to fall inside the window. The bot should have Claude fill a structured checklist for each market (one named primary source, an objective threshold, a clear timing clause, no subjective terms, no clarification notes) and let deterministic code veto on any failure.

### Operational security starts with not running anyone else's bot

"Polymarket trading bot" is one of the most heavily targeted niches for credential theft. Documented campaigns in 2025 and 2026 include a hijacked GitHub organisation hosting more than 20 malicious bot repositories ([StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/malicious-polymarket-bot-hides-in-hijacked-dev-protocol-github-org-and-steals-wallet-keys)), fake npm packages impersonating Polymarket tools ([SafeDep](https://safedep.io/malicious-polymarket-npm-crypto-wallet-drainer/)), a fake arbitrage bot whose hidden dependency harvested keys, SSH credentials and cloud logins ([SafeDep](https://safedep.io/defi-infostealer-fake-arbitrage-bot-npm/)), and 341 malicious agent skills, several of them under Polymarket-themed names ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/02/researchers-find-341-malicious-clawhub.html)). Typosquats of official package names exist on PyPI too ([GCVE](https://db.gcve.eu/vuln/MAL-2026-3220)).

The rules that follow are simple. Write the bot in this repository on top of the official `polymarket-us` package only, installed by exact name and pinned with hashes. Never clone or install a third-party Polymarket bot, skill or trading-capable connector on a machine that holds keys. Keep the API secret out of any project-local `.env` file, which is exactly where the documented stealers look; load it from an operating-system keyring or a secrets manager. Deposit only the experiment bankroll. The module that holds the key should expose one narrow function that places or cancels a limit order within pre-validated bounds, and the Claude-facing research code should run with no access to the key at all.

### Claude emits a probability; it never touches an order

Prompt injection through fetched content is unsolved. Anthropic reported a 1.4% attack success rate for Claude Opus 4.5 in browser use even with improved safeguards and called the problem "far from a solved problem" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses)). In May 2026 an attacker used an encoded message to get another vendor's model to authorise a $150,000 crypto transfer ([Giskard](https://giskard.ai/knowledge/how-grok-got-prompt-injected-an-x-user-drained-150-000-from-an-ai-wallet)). The researchers found no documented case of a prediction-market bot being manipulated through planted news, but anyone holding a position profits from text that shifts the model's estimate.

The architecture must make a successful injection nearly worthless. Claude's output is a strict schema: a probability between 0.01 and 0.99, the rules-checklist answers, cited source URLs and a short rationale. Anything that fails validation means no trade, and free text is never parsed for sizes or instructions. Retrieval is limited to an allow-list of wires, official statistical and government sites and the market's named resolution source. Code verifies that each cited URL was actually fetched in that run, injects today's date into every prompt, and rejects undated evidence. The bot runs several independent samples per market, uses the median, records the spread as its noise estimate and skips the market when samples disagree by more than about 10 points. The model version is pinned, and a model change counts as a new strategy that needs a fresh paper window. With these in place, the worst an injection can do is bias one probability, and the shrinkage weight, the 2% cap and the disagreement ceiling bound that harm.

### Circuit breakers run before every order

The cautionary case for automated trading is Knight Capital, which sent more than 4 million erroneous orders in 45 minutes in 2012 and lost about $440 million; the regulator found its controls could not stop orders that exceeded aggregate capital thresholds ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222)). The failure mode is runaway order submission, not bad forecasts.

The recommended control set runs in code the model cannot override. Every order passes pre-trade checks on price band, size caps, exposure caps, estimate age and duplicate suppression. Daily limits cap new positions (for example five) and new notional (for example 10% of bankroll). Loss limits, with positions marked at the bid, pause new entries after a 3% to 4% daily loss, pause for review after an 8% weekly loss, halve sizes at a 15% drawdown and stop the experiment at 25%. By the researchers' arithmetic a 25% drawdown has roughly a 1% probability at one-eighth Kelly if the assumed edge is real, so reaching it is strong evidence the edge is not. A kill switch cancels all orders and stops the loop, the ledger is reconciled against the exchange before every cycle, and paper and live modes share one code path with only the venue adapter swapped.

### Paper-trading gates come before any money

Realised profit cannot validate this strategy on any useful timescale. By the researchers' power calculation, showing a 5-cent net edge at 80% power takes about 600 independent bets at mid prices, and a 3-cent edge about 1,700; at three to five independent resolved bets a week that is years. A published benchmark makes the same point from another angle: detecting a 0.02 Brier edge over the market needs about 350 resolved predictions ([arXiv 2605.00420](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00420v1)).

The evaluation therefore leans on two faster measures. The first is a paired comparison of Claude's score against the market price on the same questions at the same moment, recorded for every market screened whether or not it was traded; this works mainly as a harm detector. The second is closing-line value: whether the market price moves toward the bot's position over fixed horizons after entry (24 hours, 72 hours, 7 days). In sports betting, beating the closing line is the standard early proxy for skill ([Pinnacle Odds Dropper](https://www.pinnacleoddsdropper.com/blog/closing-line-value)), and the researchers estimate it can separate a real 5-cent edge from zero in roughly 100 to 300 trades. No peer-reviewed study applies it to prediction markets, so the adaptation is their own construction.

The recommended phases, all with gates written into the repository before the first forecast so they cannot be moved afterwards, are as follows. Phase 0 is one to two weeks of data collection, plumbing and kill-switch drills with no evaluation claims. Phase 1 is paper trading for **at least eight weeks, and until at least 150 forecasts on distinct events have resolved and 200 simulated entries have 72-hour closing-line data**. Going live requires all of these: mean closing-line value above zero with a clustered t-statistic of at least 2, Claude's shrunk forecast not significantly worse than the market on Brier and log score, simulated profit after fees and book-walking of at least zero, four consecutive weeks with no reconciliation breaks or limit breaches, and no held market later disputed for a reason the checklist should have caught. Phase 2 is live trading with $200 to $500, positions of about $5 to $10, and human approval of the first 20 to 30 trades, run until 100 or more fills, with live fill prices audited against what the simulator would have assumed. Scaling proceeds in steps of at most double, to a ceiling of $2,000 to $5,000, only after 200 live trades with positive closing-line value and non-negative net profit. Shutdown triggers are significantly negative closing-line value after 100 trades, a 25% drawdown, any key or reconciliation incident, or a material platform event.

## Recommended build: a market-anchored, maker-first Claude forecaster

### The strategy

The recommendation is a single strategy with fixed guardrails, not a portfolio of ideas. Claude forecasts; the market price anchors; deterministic code decides whether and how much to trade.

The venue is Polymarket US if the in-app eligibility check passes for the account holder's state, with Kalshi as the fallback and as a second paper-traded venue for comparison, since Kalshi is cheaper for small takers and pays interest above $250. The international site is used, if reachable and permitted by its terms, only as read-only research data.

The market universe is narrow by design: binary markets resolved by one named primary source, 2 to 45 days from resolution, priced between 15 and 85 cents, with a tight spread and displayed depth of at least ten times the intended order. The bot excludes sports (the centre of the state-law fights, the category where every model lost on KellyBench, and where retail specialists do worst), short-duration crypto, insider-prone markets (military, awards, corporate announcements, personnel decisions), "mention" markets and anything with subjective wording. Weather markets are deferred: they suit a slow bot, but the forecasting there is done by numerical weather models and Claude's judgment adds little.

The pipeline runs in a fixed order. A mechanical screen applies the universe filters. Claude completes the rules checklist, and code vetoes failures. Claude then researches the question with multi-round search over the allow-list and produces five to ten independent probability estimates, which code combines by median or mean. Once enough of the bot's own forecasts have resolved, a recalibration fitted to that history adjusts the combined estimate; until then none is applied. Code blends the result with the market price at a weight of 0.3 on Claude, to be re-estimated from the bot's own data and held at or below 0.5. Code computes the edge over all-in cost, applies the 3 to 5 cent floor and the 20 to 25 cent ceiling, sizes at quarter Kelly on the blended probability under the 2% cap, and places a resting limit order with a short expiry. Positions are held to settlement unless new information reverses the estimate or a resolution-risk flag appears. Every cycle writes an append-only log of prompts, fetched documents, raw samples, book snapshots, vetoes and fills, which is both the audit trail and the evaluation dataset.

The build order follows from that. First comes the read-only market-data client and book recorder against the public gateway. Second, the paper simulator with realistic fills and fees. Third, the forecasting pipeline and decision log, with the trading key absent from the machine. Fourth, the risk engine, kill switch and reconciliation, exercised in paper mode. Only after the Phase 1 gates pass does the live adapter get a key, starting with the order-preview endpoint and tiny maker-only orders.

### Expected outcomes, stated honestly

The evidence does not support expecting a profit. Independent tests of LLMs trading with real money are negative, the positive records are short and builder-reported, and the one measured edge from combining a model with the market is about 0.005 Brier. The realistic central outcome for this design is a result near zero before model costs: the guardrails remove the well-documented ways retail accounts lose (taking longshots, paying taker fees at mid prices, trading disputed or insider-prone markets), and the shrinkage keeps Claude from betting heavily against a better-informed market. Whether Claude adds anything on top is the open question the experiment exists to answer.

Three costs cut against even that. By the researchers' arithmetic, a 4-cent net edge on a $10 position is worth about 80 cents of expected profit per trade, so the cost of several research-enabled Claude runs per market must be tracked against trading results and may exceed the edge at this bankroll. Paper results will overstate live results, as the 44-point FutureSearch gap shows. And the filters are tight: whether enough markets pass them to produce 150 to 200 independent forecasts in two to three months is unknown and should be measured in Phase 0.

The stake can still be lost entirely. At the recommended sizing that is very unlikely to come from forecasting error, because the 25% stop ends the experiment first. The remaining routes are a stolen API key, a runaway-order bug that the pre-trade checks miss, an exchange failure, or a regulatory freeze. A dedicated low balance, the kill switch and the no-third-party-code rule exist to bound those.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. The useful question is no longer whether Claude can beat Polymarket, because on the independent evidence a stand-alone LLM forecaster that trades its own opinions loses. It is whether a Claude forecast carries a sliver of information the price lacks, and whether a small, disciplined process can extract that sliver without paying more in fees, spread and model costs than it is worth. The literature says the sliver exists in blended forecasts and says nothing about whether it survives execution on a regulated US venue with a 0.095 taker coefficient. Nobody has published that test.

That makes the project's most valuable output its own forward record, not its profit. The cited fees, tiers and state lists will all go stale within weeks; a timestamped log of every forecast against the market price at that moment will not. If the paper phase shows positive closing-line value, the user will hold evidence that almost no public source has. If it does not, the gates will have stopped the experiment before any money was at risk.
